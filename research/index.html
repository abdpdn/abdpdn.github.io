Research

My research concerns the numerical study of quantum many-body systems, particularly low-dimensional lattice models in which interactions, disorder, topology, and quantum entanglement produce unconventional phases and dynamical behavior. I am interested both in identifying new quantum phases and in understanding the microscopic mechanisms that distinguish them.

Research Topics

Quantum Phase Transitions

Quantum phase transitions are qualitative changes in the ground state of a system driven by parameters such as interaction strength, hopping amplitude, magnetic field, or disorder. Unlike thermal transitions, they occur at zero temperature and arise from quantum fluctuations.

I study these transitions by examining excitation gaps, correlation functions, order parameters, entanglement entropy, and finite-size scaling. Particular attention is given to continuous transitions, Berezinskii–Kosterlitz–Thouless transitions, and transitions between insulating, ordered, localized, and topological phases.

Topological Phases in Interacting Systems

Topological phases cannot always be characterized by conventional local order parameters. Instead, they are identified through global properties such as quantized topological invariants, protected edge states, entanglement signatures, and quantized transport.

My work focuses on how particle interactions modify or generate topological phases in bosonic, fermionic, and spin systems. I use quantities such as Berry phases, winding numbers, charge pumping, edge-state densities, entanglement spectra, and nonlocal order parameters to distinguish topological phases from conventional insulating states.

Many-Body Localization

In an isolated interacting quantum system, disorder can prevent thermalization and preserve information about the initial state for very long times. This phenomenon is known as many-body localization.

I investigate localization and relaxation in disordered and quasiperiodic systems using spectral statistics, local autocorrelation functions, imbalance dynamics, entanglement growth, resonance structure, and site-resolved relaxation times. A central goal is to understand how rare or long-range resonances destabilize localized behavior and influence the crossover toward thermalization.

Non-Hermitian Quantum Matter

Non-Hermitian models provide effective descriptions of systems with gain, loss, dissipation, or asymmetric transport. Their spectra can become complex, and their eigenstates may display behavior that has no direct counterpart in conventional closed quantum systems.

I study localization, spectral transitions, non-Hermitian topology, mobility edges, and the non-Hermitian skin effect in quasiperiodic and interacting lattice models. These systems are characterized using complex-energy spectra, inverse participation ratios, winding numbers, boundary sensitivity, and real-to-complex spectral transitions.

Numerical Methods

Density-Matrix Renormalization Group and Matrix-Product States

Density-matrix renormalization group is one of the most accurate numerical methods for ground states of one-dimensional interacting systems. In the matrix-product-state formulation, the many-body wave function is represented in a compressed form that efficiently captures physically relevant entanglement.

I use these methods to compute ground-state energies, excitation gaps, density and bond profiles, correlation functions, entanglement entropy, entanglement spectra, and time evolution in large one-dimensional systems.

Exact Diagonalization

Exact diagonalization constructs the many-body Hamiltonian in a chosen basis and computes its eigenvalues and eigenvectors. It provides numerically exact results for finite systems, although the accessible system size is limited by the exponential growth of the Hilbert space.

I use full and sparse diagonalization to study spectra, eigenstates, level statistics, localization measures, quantum dynamics, correlation functions, and finite-size trends. It is particularly useful for highly excited states and nonequilibrium dynamics.

Cluster Mean-Field Theory

Cluster mean-field theory treats the quantum correlations within a finite cluster exactly while approximating the coupling between neighboring clusters through self-consistent mean fields.

I use this method to obtain approximate phase diagrams and order parameters for lattice models that are difficult to access directly at large sizes. It provides a useful compromise between single-site mean-field theory and fully many-body numerical methods.
